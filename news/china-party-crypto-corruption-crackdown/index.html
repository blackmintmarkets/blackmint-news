China's Communist Party Orders Graft Probes Into Crypto, Blockchain

The resolution adds no trading ban. It tells party investigators to chase bribes hidden in virtual currency and blockchain, and to lean on big data.

China's Communist Party has told its anti-corruption investigators to go after bribes moved through crypto. A resolution adopted Oct. 29 and published in full by state news agency Xinhua on Sunday names virtual currency and blockchain as channels of graft.

The order is aimed at officials. It is a party discipline document, so it adds no new ban and doesn't change the status of any token, Yahoo and BlockchainReporter both noted. Crypto trading is already banned on the mainland.

One sentence, four channels

In Xinhua's official English translation, the party says it will "resolutely combat corruption committed via the internet, virtual currency, cross-border capital, and blockchain." The same passage targets hidden graft, such as assets held under someone else's name and "white gloves," the middlemen who hold money for officials.

It also calls for a "big data platform for overseeing the exercise of power in the public sector," the South China Morning Post and BlockchainReporter reported. The Chinese text runs about 17,000 characters, Xinhua said.

How a crypto bribe gets traced

A blockchain is a public ledger. Every transfer between wallet addresses stays on record, so a payment can be followed years later even when wallets carry no names. The weak spot for anyone hiding money is the moment coins are swapped for cash and land in a bank account.

That is how investigators caught Yao Qian, the former head of the People's Bank of China's digital currency institute, according to a state TV documentary that aired Jan. 14. It said he took 2,000 ether in 2018 from a crypto businessman whose token sale he helped, and that investigators traced the coins to his wallet and to a 2021 sale of 370 ether for about 10 million yuan, China News Service and the SCMP reported. The documentary said Yao accepted the findings. He was expelled from the party in November 2024 and handed to prosecutors. Black Mint found no public statement from Yao or a lawyer for him.

In our opinion: the crypto clause is one sentence, but it sits in a resolution adopted by the full Central Committee, and the Yao case shows the playbook: follow the coins until they touch a bank. The risk falls on officials and their fixers who parked wealth in tokens. The thing to watch is whether discipline commissions start citing crypto in published case notices.
