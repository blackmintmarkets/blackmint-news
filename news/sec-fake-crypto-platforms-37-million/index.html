SEC Says Eight Firms Ran Fake Crypto Platforms, Taking $37 Million

The SEC complaint says social media ads led investors into group chats, then to fake trading sites backed by forged SEC certificates.

The Securities and Exchange Commission on Thursday charged eight Colorado-based entities with taking more than $37 million from U.S. investors through fake crypto trading platforms, the agency said. The SEC said the people running them were likely overseas.

These are allegations in a civil complaint filed in federal court in Colorado, and none has been proven. The SEC's announcement included no response from the defendants.

How the SEC says the scheme worked

According to the complaint, social media ads steered investors into group and private chats. People in those chats posed as staff of an investment adviser. They first gave tips on trading U.S. stocks, the SEC said, then pushed victims to send as much money as possible to a supposed crypto trading platform.

The SEC said the adviser and the exchange were both fake, even as investors were led to believe they were buying real crypto. To look legitimate, the operators allegedly made fake SEC certificates and filed paperwork with the SEC, FINRA and FinCEN. Then they pointed doubtful investors to those filings.

Filing a form with a regulator isn't the same as being approved by one. A filing on its own doesn't mean the agency vetted the business.

Four pairs, swapped every few months

The scheme ran from August 2024 through May 2026 as four adviser-and-exchange pairs, the agency said. Each pair ran for a few months before the operators moved on to the next one.

The eight defendants are Advisor WeChat Capital, Tech Brokerage Asset Alliance Ltd., HB Wealth Advisors, IdeaCrypto Exchange LLC, Convergent Wealth Advisors Inc., RealSimple Crypto Exchange Inc., Associated Wealth Advisors Ltd. and Essential Crypto Exchange. The SEC alleges fraud under the Securities Exchange Act, plus Investment Advisers Act violations for some. It wants injunctions, repayment with interest, and civil penalties.

"The SEC continues to seek out and expose fraudulent investment scams that prey on U.S. investors," said Laura D'Allaird, who leads the SEC's Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit. Regulators in Hong Kong, Singapore, China and Switzerland helped with the case, the SEC said.

In our opinion: the detail worth noting is the fake paperwork. These operators allegedly used real regulators' names to win trust, and kept changing brands every few months. The SEC says investors can check anyone selling an investment on Investor.gov. If the operators really are overseas, getting the $37 million back will be the hard part.
