Japan's Producer Prices Rise 7.1% as Fuel and Metals Costs Climb

October's reading beat forecasts by 0.6 point and tops September's 6.8%. Copper-linked metals rose 31.7%, as the BOJ weighs its 1.25% rate.

Japanese companies paid 7.1% more for goods in October than a year earlier, up from 6.8% in September, the Bank of Japan said Thursday. That beat the private-sector forecast by 0.6 percentage point, Nikkei reported.

The figure came out at 8:50 a.m. Tokyo time, or 6:50 p.m. ET Wednesday. It lands on a central bank that held its policy rate at 1.25% last month, with one board member voting for 1.5%, its October statement shows.

What this index tracks

The corporate goods price index measures what companies charge each other for goods, from steel to fuel, before anything reaches a store. It's read as an early guide to consumer prices, since business costs tend to show up on shelves later, as Sankei and Nikkei noted. Prices rose 0.7% from September alone.

Fuel, chemicals and metals led

The BOJ release and both newspapers show:
- Petroleum and coal products: up 15.9% from a year earlier
- Chemicals: up 15.5%
- Nonferrous metals, including copper: up 31.7%
- Farm, forestry and fishery products: down 25.0%

Import prices in yen were 24.8% higher than a year ago. Nikkei tied the fuel rise to higher crude prices since the summer, and the copper gain to demand from data centers.

Why a Tokyo price report reaches crypto

A BOJ rate hike makes it more expensive to borrow in yen, including for investors outside Japan who fund trades in other assets with yen. The board's next policy meeting is Dec. 17-18, per its meeting calendar.

Bitcoin was near $73,680 at 11:59 p.m. ET Wednesday, roughly flat over 24 hours, per CoinGecko.

In our opinion: the gap worth watching is 7.1% wholesale inflation against a 1.25% policy rate, with one vote for a hike already on the record in October. Companies are eating higher fuel and metal costs now. How much of that reaches shoppers in Japan's next consumer price report is the question the board carries into Dec. 17.
