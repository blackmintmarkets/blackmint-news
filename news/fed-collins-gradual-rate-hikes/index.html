Fed's Collins Backs 'Steady, Gradual' Hikes Ahead of Dec. 8-9 Meeting

The Boston Fed chief said recent price data and a firmer job market mean rates over the next year could end up 'a bit higher' than she thought.

Boston Fed President Susan Collins said on Saturday she favors modest rate increases taken one small step at a time, with the Fed's target range at 3.75% to 4% and its next decision due Dec. 8-9.

"A steady, gradual path seems appropriate to me at the moment. But I'm open-minded," Collins told reporters at a Boston Fed housing conference, Reuters reported.

For crypto holders, the Fed's pace sets the return on cash. Each hike raises what Treasury bills and money-market funds pay, and bitcoin pays nothing by comparison.

Why she argued against a faster pace

Collins said she sees the risks as two-sided. Inflation is high and spreading through the economy, she said, while the job market is stable with room to speed up. She also said the rise in longer-term borrowing costs this year could weigh on demand more than she expects. Taken together, she said, that argues against speeding up, according to Reuters and Investing.com.

She added that gradual "doesn't necessarily mean a particular cadence," and that price data from the past three months has pushed her expected rate path for the next year "a bit higher" than she thought a couple of months ago.

How a hike works

The federal funds rate is what banks charge each other for overnight loans. A quarter-point increase in December would lift the range to 4% to 4.25%. Reuters reported the Fed last raised rates in September, and that markets expect quarter-point moves in December and again in January. Bloomberg, cited by Finansavisen, reported that market pricing points to one more hike in March or April.

Not every official agrees on size. Reuters said Fed Governor Christopher Waller's recent comments raised expectations that some officials want a bigger move next month.

Bitcoin was near $63,760 at 5:48 p.m. ET, down 6.7% in 24 hours, per CoinGecko.

In our opinion: the real argument inside the Fed is no longer whether to hike, but by how much. Collins wants small steps; Waller's remarks pointed the other way. With inflation above the 2% target for more than five years, per Reuters, the Dec. 8-9 statement is where that debate gets settled, at least until January.
