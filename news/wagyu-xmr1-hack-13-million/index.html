Wagyu's XMR1 Token Falls 99% After Attacker Sells $13.5 Million

An account holding XMR1's deployer key moved 5.2 million tokens and sold part of them on Hyperliquid, Bitquery said. Wagyu's founder blamed a hacked web host and pledged refunds.

XMR1, a token that stands in for Monero on the Hyperliquid exchange, fell from about $500 to about $1 on Friday, Nov. 20, after an attacker sold 369,623 tokens to 420 buyers for $13.51 million, according to an investigation by onchain data firm Bitquery. Decrypt also reported the drop of more than 99%.

How the token works

Monero is a privacy coin that runs on its own blockchain, so it can't trade directly on Hyperliquid. Wagyu, a cross-chain swap service, fills that gap: users deposit Monero and receive XMR1, which they can later redeem for real Monero. That makes XMR1 a "wrapped" token, and its value depends on the issuer's reserves and security holding up.

Bitquery said the account that deployed XMR1 sent 5.2 million tokens to an account created that morning, which then sold into the market. The proceeds were moved to Arbitrum, swapped for ether and mostly bridged to Ethereum, and about 7,348 ETH sat in three wallets at Bitquery's last check. Native Monero was not affected, Decrypt reported.

The web host link

Wagyu's pseudonymous founder, known as PerpetualCow, said the token's deployer credentials were stolen from 1984.hosting, an Icelandic web host, and that he would personally refund buyers who lost money and relaunch Wagyu, according to Bitquery and Decrypt. He did not give a timeline.

1984 ehf., which runs the host, said in a joint statement with Iceland's national cyber response team, CERT-IS, that it suffered a serious and technically advanced attack and temporarily shut down its services, DV reported. Its managing director told RÚV that well-funded attackers were behind it. The statement did not mention Wagyu.

Bitquery said onchain data alone can't show whether the credentials were stolen, and that it can't see how much Monero backs the remaining XMR1.

In our opinion: the 420 buyers on the other side of those sales carry the loss until a refund actually arrives, so the refund is the number to watch. The deeper lesson is that a wrapped token is only as safe as the credentials that control it, and here those appear to have outlived the server they sat on. Holders of other Wagyu tokens, including ZEC1, which Bitquery says the same deployer account still controls, are the next group with a stake in the post-mortem.
