Coinone Says Support Contractor Breach Exposed Customer ID Numbers

Passwords, bank details and national ID numbers shared with Coinone's help desk may have leaked. The exchange says its trading and wallet systems were separate.

Coinone customers who used the South Korean exchange's help desk may have had passwords, bank account numbers and national ID numbers exposed, the exchange said in a notice posted at 6 p.m. ET Saturday. The breach hit MetaM, the outside firm that runs its customer support.

An attacker got into MetaM's servers around 2 p.m. ET on Nov. 11 (4 a.m. Nov. 12 in Seoul) and viewed or took stored support records, Coinone's notice says. Customers who never used chat or phone support are not affected.

What sat in the support logs

A support log holds whatever a customer typed or said to an agent. Coinone's list of items at risk: phone number, name, email, birth date, bank account, password, resident registration number (South Korea's national ID), ID issue date, home address, wallet address and transaction ID. The leaked items differ by conversation. Coinone said it plans a lookup service so each customer can check.

How many people are affected? The notice doesn't say, and The Asia Business Daily and ChosunBiz reports give no count.

Trading and wallet systems run apart

Coinone said its accounts, trading and wallet systems are separate from MetaM's servers and show no sign of intrusion. Every login needs a second check (an OTP code or KakaoTalk verification on the owner's phone), so the leaked details alone can't be used to log in or withdraw, the exchange said.

It moved outsourced support to email only and reported the incident to the Korea Internet & Security Agency, the Personal Information Protection Commission and the Financial Supervisory Service. MetaM said in its own Nov. 14 notice that not every user's data leaked, Yonhap reported.

What Coinone asked customers to do

Anyone who shared a password with support should change it there and anywhere it's reused, Coinone said. It never asks for passwords, OTP numbers or verification codes, or for withdrawals, by phone, text or email. Under Korean privacy law, customers who can't prove an exact loss can seek statutory damages of up to 3 million won, the notice says.

In our opinion: the leak came through a help desk, and the risk now is impersonation. The notice still leaves out the number of people in the leaked logs and the date its lookup service opens. Until then, a call citing an old support chat deserves suspicion, as Coinone itself warned.
